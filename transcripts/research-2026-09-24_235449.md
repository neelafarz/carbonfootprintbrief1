## 3. Why published figures conflict

### Different things are being counted

A footprint may include just the processor, the whole server, cooling, electricity production, hardware manufacturing, training, networks, or the user’s device.

Google’s carbon method includes some embodied hardware emissions, so describing it as “electricity only” would also be inaccurate. Its water calculation, however, uses data-centre water consumption rather than the full upstream water footprint. [Google methodology](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.15734v1)

### Water has several meanings

- **Withdrawal:** water taken from a source, some of which may be returned.
- **Consumption:** water not returned for immediate local reuse, often because it evaporates.
- **Indirect water:** water used to generate electricity or manufacture equipment.
- **Local water stress:** how consequential that consumption is where and when it occurs.

The “bottle of water for a conversation” literature uses modelled assumptions about deployment and water use. It should not be carried forward as a universal rate for current AI products. [Li et al., *Making AI Less Thirsty*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03271)

### Carbon depends on accounting choices

Electricity use and carbon emissions are separate quantities. The same energy can have different emissions depending on its source.

“Location-based” accounting uses the local electricity mix. “Market-based” accounting also reflects electricity purchasing arrangements. These should be labelled separately rather than silently mixed.

### Benchmark quality varies

Company measurements offer real operational data but limited outside reproducibility. Academic benchmarks offer controlled comparisons but may poorly represent commercial deployment.

Even primary sources need checking: the exploratory video benchmark contains an incorrect light-bulb analogy—**0.14 Wh at 10 W is about 50 seconds, not 50 minutes**. Its numerical results warrant more weight than its everyday equivalences. [Benchmark](https://huggingface.co/blog/jdelavande/text-to-video-energy-cost)

## 4. A defensible project methodology

My recommendation is to start with the **finished deliverable**: a report, campaign, video, or resolved coding task.

1. **Count the whole workflow.** Include revisions, rejected images, discarded clips, coding-agent attempts, tests, and review.
2. **Record meaningful settings.** Text length and reasoning mode; image resolution and variants; video duration and resolution; coding tool calls and execution.
3. **Separate components.** AI serving, devices, networks, and allocated training/manufacturing should remain identifiable.
4. **Prefer direct measurements or matching provider data.** Use benchmarks as explicitly labelled proxies.
5. **Calculate coherent low, central, and high scenarios.** Vary settings, attempts, and infrastructure assumptions. These are scenario ranges unless statistical confidence is actually established.
6. **Report both total impact and impact per accepted output.** Efficient individual attempts can still produce an inefficient project.

The basic arithmetic is:

**Project energy = sum of all activity counts × matching energy factors, plus other included energy use.**

Carbon then requires matching electricity factors and any separately allocated lifecycle emissions. Water requires its own direct and indirect factors.

### Illustrative project totals

These examples demonstrate arithmetic, **not measured professional averages**:

| Example | Calculation | Result and boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Report with 100 text requests | 100 × Google’s 0.24 Wh reference | **24 Wh serving energy**, assuming every request matches that reference; this is not a forecast derived from the median. |
| Campaign with 20 accepted images and 5 attempts each | 100 × 2.907 Wh | Approximately **0.29 kWh**, using the academic benchmark proxy. |
| Video with 12 accepted clips and 10 attempts each | 120 × an assumed 10–100 Wh/attempt | **1.2–12 kWh**, a sensitivity scenario before additional production work. |
| Coding project | All model calls + execution/tests + review | **No defensible generic total yet**; it needs a workload trace or measured pilot. |

For scale, **1 kWh = 1,000 Wh**. If a project used 1.2–12 kWh and we *assumed* electricity at 400 g CO₂e/kWh, electricity emissions would be **0.48–4.8 kg CO₂e**. That is an illustrative conversion, not a claim about any provider.
